# What makes a CV effective, and how conventions differ across the 7 markets

Research for issue #7 (feeds #14, the Quality Score measures). Gathered 2026-11-08 from web searches and
search-result summaries. Several primary pages were not opened in full (marked "not verified at source").
Figures from papers come from abstracts or institutional summaries, so check them against the paper before
quoting them in the product.

## Evidence strength scale

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| study | Peer-reviewed or working-paper field experiment or survey with a stated method |
| survey | Opinion survey of recruiters or executives; says what they report, not what works |
| vendor | Commercial company's own study; method partly documented, conflict of interest |
| official | Government or EU guidance; authoritative for conventions and law, not for effectiveness |
| convention | Repeated in guides and blogs; no source traced back to data |

## Bottom line

1. Very little CV advice is backed by causal evidence. The strongest evidence is about what hurts: spelling
   errors, long unemployment gaps, and signals that trigger discrimination (names, photo, age, gender).
2. The popular "7 seconds" and "ATS rejects 75%" claims are weak or unsupported (see below).
3. Quantified achievements, action verbs and keyword density are heavily promoted but have no controlled
   study that we could find. They are reasonable as advice, poor as heavily weighted score components.
4. Conventions differ most on personal data (photo, age, gender, nationality, marital status), document type
   (Japan's two documents) and length. A single English-derived rubric would mark correct CVs in several
   markets as defective.

## Candidate Evaluative Measures

"Market variation" lists only where the measure changes by CV Language. "All" means it applies unchanged.

| Measure | Evidence | Strength | Market variation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spelling and typo rate | Scenario experiment with 445 recruiters: many errors cut interview probability by about 18.5 points, few errors by about 7.3; about half the penalty works through inferred conscientiousness, interpersonal skill and ability (Sterkens et al., IZA DP 14614). Other studies agree in direction; one earlier one found no penalty in one of two experiments. Penalty weakens among recruiters who spell badly themselves. | study | All. Needs a per-language spell and grammar check, which is weak for ar, ja, zh. |
| Employment gap length and recency of last role | Field experiment, about 12,000 fictitious resumes: callbacks fell about 45% between 1 and 8 months unemployed, most of the drop in the first 8 months; effect larger in tight labour markets (Kroft, Lange, Notowidigdo, QJE 2013). US, three job families, mostly early-career profiles. | study | Date formats differ per language (Japan uses era years, wareki). Gap tolerance is unmeasured outside the US. |
| Length (1 to 2 pages) | Simulation with 482 recruiters: two-page chosen 2.3x more overall, 1.4x entry level, 2.9x managerial (ResumeGo 2018). A resume-writing company ran it, not peer-reviewed. | vendor | Convention: 1 page common in France and for juniors, 2 acceptable in Spain and Latin America. Japan's shokumu keirekisho is a separate document. Score as "length fits experience level", not a fixed page cap. |
| Readable layout and clear section headings | MHLW/Hello Work guidance says layout and type size affect whether the reader goes on (official, Japan). Ladders 2018 eye-tracking says clear structure helps scanning. | official / vendor | Headings per language are the Language Pack's job. |
| Parseable file (text layer, simple structure) | ATS vendors say parsing errors force manual review or re-entry. Only third-party blogs were retrieved, vendor docs not opened. | vendor (secondhand) | Arabic and CJK may parse worse in some ATS; the evidence is a vendor blog, not tested. |
| Contact details present and complete | Convention; no study. | convention | Required fields differ (see below). |
| Quantified achievements | "40% more callbacks" circulates but traces to vendor analyses (TalentWorks via ERE 2019, Jobscan) with unclear method. No controlled study found. | convention / vendor | All; verbs and number formats are language-specific. |
| Action verbs | Only practitioner verb lists found. No controlled comparison found. | convention | Likely less meaningful in ja and zh (inference, not sourced). |
| Skills section and keyword match to the job | Hidden Workers report (HBS and Accenture 2021, survey of 8,720 workers and 2,275 executives in US, UK, Germany) argues ATS filters on missing qualifiers exclude qualified people. Supports Fit Score matching, not generic skill counts. | survey | Skill vocabulary needs per-language aliases (Language Pack). |
| Word-count band (current code: 300 to 800 words) | No evidence for the specific band. | none | n/a |
| Bullet usage | No evidence. | convention | n/a |
| Personal data handling (age, gender, marital status, photo where not expected) | Anonymisation experiments are mixed. French experiment: anonymous CVs widened the interview gap for minority candidates from about 2.4 to about 13 points (Behaghel et al., AEJ Applied 2015). Name-based callback gap about 50% in the US (Bertrand and Mullainathan, AER 2004). | study | Strongest market variation. Treat as an advisory flag, never a quality deduction. |

## Popular claims that lack support

- **"Recruiters spend 6 to 7.4 seconds on a CV."** Ladders ran eye-tracking in 2012 (30 recruiters, per secondary
  accounts) and 2018. It is a commercial study, not peer-reviewed. ERE criticises the 2018 report for not stating
  the number of recruiters, their experience or the number of repetitions. Use only as "first pass is short",
  not as a number.
- **"ATS software auto-rejects most CVs."** Vendor accounts say rejection comes from employer-set knockout
  questions and rules, and that scores only order the queue. Only blog summaries were retrieved, not vendor
  docs. The HBS and Accenture report is a better source for the opposite risk: employers configure filters
  that exclude qualified people. Net: parse quality matters, but "ATS score" is not a real thing to promise.
- **Quantified achievements and action verbs "increase callbacks by X%".** No study traced.
- **"Two pages are always better."** One vendor simulation.

## Conventions by CV Language

Mostly convention and official guidance. Photo and personal data are the items that change what a CV analyser
should flag.

### English (en)
- Photo, date of birth, marital status and nationality are normally left out (UK and US norm). A photo can be
  read as a negative signal in this market. Sources: guide summaries, not official; not verified at source.
- 1 page early career, 2 for experienced is the usual advice; evidence is the vendor study above.

### French (fr)
- Photo is neither required nor forbidden. Asking for one can be seen as discriminatory per press summaries.
  Not verified against Legifrance or the Defenseur des droits.
- Anonymous CV: a 2006 law planned it for firms with 50 or more employees but it was never implemented for lack
  of a decree; a site claims it became optional in 2015 (not verified in official text).
- The French experiment (Behaghel et al. 2015) found anonymising hurt minority candidates in that setting.
  Do not assume less personal data is always better.

### German (de)
- Photo is voluntary; no legal duty to supply one, and sources say an employer should not require it under the
  AGG. The Antidiskriminierungsstelle recommends omitting photo, age, gender and birthplace (second-hand
  citation; original page not opened). Practice still often includes a photo; the share quoted (80 to 90% of
  recruiters) is unreliable.
- A tabular, dated, reverse-chronological Lebenslauf is the norm (convention).

### Spanish (es)
- Photo common in Spain and Latin America, not mandatory. 1 to 2 pages. Date of birth, nationality and
  sometimes DNI/NIE reference are often included. Europass is accepted in Spain; SEPE publishes CV guidance
  (its content was not retrieved). Latin America: no official or detailed source found; country differences
  unknown.

### Arabic (ar) and Gulf markets
- No formal study of employer expectations found. Vendor blogs say a photo is expected by most Gulf employers,
  nationality is standard (visa sponsorship), date of birth often included, marital status disputed between
  sources. The claim that employers want separate English and Arabic CVs is vendor-only.
- Parsing risk: right-to-left text and Arabic shaping in PDFs. One vendor blog claims ATS failure in MENA;
  untested here. Needs its own validation set, as the map already requires.

### Japanese (ja)
- Two documents: rirekisho (history, standard form) and shokumu keirekisho (work history in free format, written
  to show practical ability, per the MHLW and Hello Work pamphlet). The pamphlet says to add what the rirekisho
  cannot hold, and that format is free.
- Photo is part of the traditional rirekisho. The government form issued by MHLW in April 2021 (after the
  Japanese Standards Association withdrew its JIS-based sample in July 2020) makes the gender field optional.
  Employers are asked to refer to it.
- A length of 1 to 2 A4 sheets for the shokumu keirekisho was not found in official material; do not hard-code.
- Dates: era years (wareki) appear on forms; a date parser needs both.

### Chinese (zh)
- Photo, age, marital status and sometimes number of children appear in practice according to one resident
  account (anecdote). The Employment Promotion Law (2008) prohibits discrimination on gender, ethnicity and
  others; sources conflict on age. No official guidance on CV photos found.
- No source found on CV length, format or ATS usage in China. Treat as open.

### Europass (cross-market)
- An older Europass instruction sheet marks the photo as optional ("if requested"); the online tool supports
  photo, date of birth and nationality, and drops empty fields. A current official page was not retrieved
  (sources were guides and an older EEAS copy).

## Critique of the current scoring (docs/scoring.md)

Compared with the evidence above:

- **Nothing in the current model measures spelling or gaps**, the two items with the best experimental support.
  Both are measurable by rules (spell check per language; date arithmetic on detected roles).
- **Skills count (8 points each, saturating at 13)** rewards listing, not relevance. No evidence supports a
  count; the only supported use of skills is matching to a target, which belongs in the Fit Score.
- **Experience level base (Fresher 40 to Senior 100)** scores seniority, not CV quality. A well-written junior
  CV is capped. Seniority also gives no information about how to improve the CV.
- **Word-count band (300 to 800) and bullet bonus (+15)** have no evidence. The only length evidence is a
  vendor study that favours 2 pages for experienced candidates, which sits partly outside this band.
- **Structure (12 per detected section)** matches the sound idea of clear headings, but the section list is
  English only; headings in the other 6 languages need Language Packs or every non-English CV is penalised.
- **Career alignment (70 plus 5 per exact keyword)** is a Fit measure presented as part of the overall score.
  Without a Target it measures nothing real.
- **Personal data** is neither detected nor treated per market. A US-style rule "remove the photo" would be
  wrong for ja, ar-Gulf and es.
- Output saturates near 90 to 99 for good CVs (per the 2026-11-08 run), so the score cannot rank good against
  excellent. Evidence-backed measures that vary between CVs (errors, gaps, parseability) help here.

## Suggested direction for #14 (not a decision)

- Lead the Quality Score with measures that have study-level support: spelling and grammar rate, gap and
  recency checks, parseability.
- Keep convention-level measures (headings present, bullets, quantified results, length fit) but with low
  weight, and say in the Advice that they are conventions, not proven effects.
- Move skills match and career alignment to the Fit Score.
- Make personal-data items market-aware flags with no score effect.
- Surface the evidence strength per measure in the explanation, which suits the glossary's "explainable".

## Open questions

1. Official sources not yet opened: current Europass pages, Legifrance and Defenseur des droits on the photo,
   antidiskriminierungsstelle.de, SEPE guidance, Greenhouse and Lever help centres.
2. Do any ATS actually handle Arabic and CJK text worse? Needs a test on real parsers, not vendor blogs.
3. Gap tolerance and recency effects outside the US (the only field experiment found is US).
4. Photo effects on callbacks in Japan, China, the Gulf and Latin America: none found.
5. Chinese CV length, format and screening practice: no source found.
6. Whether per-language spell and grammar checkers are accurate enough for ar, ja, zh to score on (belongs with
   the NLP ticket).
7. Recruiter-side validity: do recruiters in each market weight the same signals? Surveys found cover the US,
   Canada and Europe only.
